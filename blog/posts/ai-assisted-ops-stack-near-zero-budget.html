Running an AI-assisted ops stack on a near-zero budget

Stefan YasinAug 28, 2026~5 min read

There's a pattern I keep seeing in the marketing world: people subscribe to eight tools because each one does one thing, and the stack quietly runs to $1,000 or $2,000 a month. The better pattern is older than software: you don't need eight tools, you need three tools that actually talk to each other. My ops stack follows that rule, and it costs almost nothing.

What the stack is

I run an agent-based automation layer on my own hardware: a local orchestrator with model access through cheap APIs, plus the monitoring tools I've built (endpointwatch, pinggrid, rackpulse). Three layers, all talking to each other:

  • Collect. The monitors watch servers, endpoints, and the network continuously, and emit structured events.
  • Reason. An agent layer turns those events into plain-language assessments and actions. It's the difference between "port 443 down" and "the website is down, the certificate expires in 6 days, and here's who to call".
  • Act. Scheduled pipelines handle the repetitive work: ingesting email into searchable notes, tracking application statuses, transcribing meeting and video content, generating daily digests.

What it costs

Near zero. Local models cover the private work, cheap API access covers the heavy lifting, and everything runs on hardware I already own. Compare that with the subscription stacks I see quoted in the same conversations, and the difference is two orders of magnitude, with better privacy: the sensitive stuff never leaves the building.

What it actually does for me

  • My job hunt runs on it. Postings are scanned continuously, applications are tracked, cover letters and resumes are tailored per role from a master profile. The repetitive parts are automated; the judgment stays mine.
  • My monitoring writes its own alerts. The LLM layer converts raw events into plain-English summaries, which is the difference between an alert that gets ignored and one that gets acted on.
  • Email becomes a knowledge base. Incoming material is captured, filed, and searchable instead of sitting in an inbox.
  • Content compounds. The same pipelines transcribe and summarize the material I read, so nothing I consume is wasted.

The lessons

  • Integration beats inventory. Three tools that share state outperform eight tools that don't. Every subscription should justify its data flow, not just its feature list.
  • Automation is a force multiplier for a solo operator. What I can't do is hire a team, so the stack works shifts I don't.
  • Privacy is a feature of local-first. Running the reasoning layer locally means my data is mine, and in a customer-facing ops role that's a story worth telling.
  • Cheap models are good enough for structured work. The frontier models are impressive, but most operational workloads are bounded tasks where a small model with the right context wins.

None of this is exotic. It's the same discipline as the incident-response post: collect everything, decide what matters, tell the person who can act. The only difference is that now the person who can act sometimes runs on my own hardware.
