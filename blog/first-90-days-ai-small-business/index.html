The first 90 days of AI in a small business

A calendar, not a demo. Three months of boring automations that pay for themselves before the chatbots arrive.

I keep hearing the same story: a business buys an AI chatbot, points it at their website, and waits for the savings. Six months later the chatbot is still answering “what are your hours” and the owner has decided AI was hype.

AI was not the problem. The order was.

Small businesses do not need intelligence first. They need their repetitive work to stop eating their week. The first 90 days of AI should be a calendar, not a demo.

Month one: paper and inbox. Point an automation at the boring intake work. Receipts, invoices, emails that ask the same three questions, documents that need filing under a name and a date. This is not glamorous. It pays immediately, because it removes the work nobody wants to do and everyone does badly at 4pm on a Friday.

Month two: first drafts, human review. Draft the responses, the quotes, the FAQ answers, the meeting summaries. A human reads and sends every one. You are buying time on the thinking part, not the judgment part. The moment you skip the review, the savings stop being savings.

Month three: the digest. One automated report a week: what arrived, what was paid, what has not been answered, what is trending wrong. Now the AI is not doing a task. It is giving the owner their week back in one page.

What to skip until this works: chatbots on the website, agents with access to your accounts, anything that promises to run your business. Those are month ten tools wearing month one clothes. If the boring automations did not pay, the clever ones will not either.

The test is money. At day 90, ask one question: what did this replace? If the answer is “a demo,” start again with the receipts.

This is the same playbook I wrote about in AI for small business: start with the boring stuff, and it is the one we run at Cloud and Cable. The bots come later. The boring stuff pays first.
