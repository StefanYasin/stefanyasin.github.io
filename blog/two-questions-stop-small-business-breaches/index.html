The two questions that stop most small business breaches

Stored credentials and untested backups cause more damage than exotic malware. Two boring questions fix most of it.

I recently ran an audit on a working machine, the kind a small business owner uses every day. The scan found over thirty stored credentials sitting in the operating system’s credential vault, including old ones for systems that no longer exist. Nothing exotic. Just years of “remember this password.”

Now imagine what an AI agent, or any tool with too much access, does when it finds thirty saved passwords. It does not need to be clever. It just needs to be running as you.

Small business breaches rarely come from exotic malware. They come from three boring things: credentials left lying around, backups that were never tested, and software that stopped getting patched because nobody owned the job. I wrote about the backups in backups are a promise you have not tested. This is the credentials part.

The first question: who can touch what? If an employee leaves, does every password they ever saw get changed? If a tool runs as the owner, can it reach the bank login? Most small businesses answer “probably” to both. Probably is how breaches happen.

The second question: do we know what is running? On the machine I audited, services were listening on the network that nobody had opened on purpose, and startup programs were running from folders any user could write to. Each one is a doorway with the door wedged open.

Neither question needs a security team. They need one boring audit and one boring habit. Review stored credentials quarterly and delete what you do not use. Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it exists, because a stolen password stops mattering when there is a second door. Patch on a schedule, not when you remember.

I put the audit into an open source tool, agent-surface-report, on GitHub. It answers both questions in one pass and prints what to do about each finding.

The expensive breach is not the one in the news. It is the one your business absorbs quietly: the drained account, the locked files, the client who leaves. Two questions, asked quarterly, prevent most of them. Ask them before a tool asks them for you.

Written by Stefan Yasin, Hamilton, Ontario.
